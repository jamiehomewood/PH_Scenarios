Scenario 1: A Day in the Life: Margaret's Journey

Follow Margaret, 78, recently widowed with COPD and mobility problems, from home through GP, hospital, discharge and back into the community. (about 90 minutes)

Learning outcomes

  1. Identify health and social needs that interact across a care pathway
  2. Explain how gaps at handover points (transitions) can lead to readmission
  3. Map the professionals and community assets who should be involved
  4. Apply the wider determinants of health to one person's experience
Content note: Bereavement and loneliness. Some students may have recent experience of losing a grandparent.

Reveal 1
Her husband Frank died eight months ago. He managed the money and did the driving.
Reveal 2
She used to go to a Tuesday lunch club. She hasn't been since the funeral.
Reveal 3
Two inhalers are on the kitchen side. One is empty. She isn't sure which is which.
Reveal 4
She's sleeping in the chair downstairs because the stairs make her too breathless.
Facilitator notes
  • Look for: a cold home (fuel poverty makes COPD worse), rugs and clutter (falls risk), stairs, one-person meals, sympathy cards.
  • Loneliness and social isolation are recognised risk factors for poorer physical and mental health.
  • Confusion about inhalers is a sign of low health literacy. Nobody has checked her inhaler technique.
  • Losing Frank also meant losing his roles: finances, transport, shared tasks. This is a hidden social need.

Who needs to be involved?

What barriers might she experience?

What could prevent hospital readmission?

Reveal 1
It took three weeks to get this appointment. She rang at 8am on four different days.
Reveal 2
Reception suggested she "use the NHS App next time". She doesn't have a smartphone.
Reveal 3
In a 10-minute consultation she mentions, as she's leaving, that she's "not sleeping much".
Reveal 4
She missed her annual COPD review last year. Frank used to drive her.
Facilitator notes
  • Digital exclusion and access barriers are an example of the inverse care law (Tudor Hart, 1971).
  • A "doorknob" disclosure may point to grief, low mood or anxiety. This is a Making Every Contact Count (MECC) opportunity.
  • Referral options: social prescribing link worker, pulmonary rehabilitation, an inhaler technique check, and flu, pneumococcal and COVID vaccinations.
  • Transport is a determinant of health. Losing a driver can mean losing access to care.

What could prevent hospital readmission?

Reveal 1
She's admitted with an infective exacerbation of COPD.
Reveal 2
On her second night she becomes confused and agitated. Staff suspect delirium.
Reveal 3
She keeps asking who is feeding her cat.
Reveal 4
The next-of-kin field is blank. Her son lives 200 miles away and doesn't know she's in hospital.
Facilitator notes
  • Delirium is common in older inpatients. It is distressing and linked to longer stays and poorer outcomes.
  • Deconditioning: older people can lose mobility quickly during bed rest. Get her moving early.
  • The cat stands for the practical worries that stop people accepting admission or push them to leave early.
  • Discharge planning should begin on admission, not on the day she leaves.

What can you observe?

What are Margaret's health needs?

What are her social needs?

What might professionals miss?

Who needs to be involved?

What barriers might she experience?

What could prevent hospital readmission?

Reveal 1
It's Friday at 6pm. She waited four hours for her medication from pharmacy.
Reveal 2
She has been shown a new inhaler technique once, while she was still recovering from delirium.
Reveal 3
The discharge letter went to the GP. Nobody told the lunch club, a neighbour or her son.
Reveal 4
She is going home by hospital transport to a house where the heating has been off for five days.
Facilitator notes
  • Late Friday discharges happen when community services are closing for the weekend, which raises the risk of readmission.
  • Models to discuss: Discharge to Assess / Home First, reablement, and the community pharmacy Discharge Medicines Service.
  • Teaching given during delirium is unlikely to be remembered. Use teach-back once she has recovered.
  • Who is the named coordinator? Handovers between services are where needs go missing.

What could prevent hospital readmission?

Reveal 1
The fridge has half a pint of milk that is out of date.
Reveal 2
She has turned the heating down to save money.
Reveal 3
A neighbour pops round now and then, but doesn't know Margaret has been in hospital.
Reveal 4
She's frightened of another "attack" and decides to stay in the chair all weekend.
Facilitator notes
  • Readmission risks: cold home, poor nutrition, medication confusion, immobility, fear and anxiety, no follow-up in place.
  • Ask what would need to be in place at 6pm on Friday for this to go differently.
  • Fear of breathlessness leads to avoiding activity, which leads to deconditioning and more breathlessness. Pulmonary rehab and anxiety support can break the cycle.

What would Margaret say matters most to her?

What could prevent hospital readmission?

Reveal 1
Available locally: a social prescribing link worker, district nurses, pulmonary rehab, Age UK befriending, a lunch club with transport.
Reveal 2
Financial help she may be entitled to but has not claimed: Attendance Allowance, and the Warm Home Discount via her energy supplier.
Reveal 3
Margaret says: "I just want to be able to get to the shops and have someone to talk to."
Facilitator notes
  • Build an ecomap on the board, with Margaret at the centre.
  • Care Act 2014 wellbeing principle and assessment of needs. Personalisation means asking "what matters to you?", not "what's the matter with you?"
  • Asset-based approach: the lunch club, the neighbour and her son are assets as well as formal services.
  • Public health angle: many of her needs are social and economic rather than clinical.
